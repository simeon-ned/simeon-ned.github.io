A Reactive Generative Motion Layer for Unified Whole-Body Controllers

A proprioception-conditioned generative interface to a unified whole-body controller.

  6. References

Unified whole-body controllers can already track rich and diverse motions once provided with a dense reference. What remains open — and challenging — is the fast, reactive generation of such references from sparse intent and task targets.

A joystick, full-body teleoperation interface, or higher-level foundation model must ultimately be converted into a trackable whole-body reference at 50–200 Hz, conditioned on the robot's actual state. Purely kinematic generators instead produce motion clips in an idealized world. After a push, a payload change, or sensor noise, such a clip may already be inconsistent with the physical system, and a tracker that follows it blindly can be neither maximally agile nor robust.

I propose a Motion Layer: a compact generative model that maps sparse intent and a short proprioceptive history to the next whole-body reference, at the tracker rate of 50–200 Hz, while the unified controller remains frozen. In the Helix notation this occupies an S0.5 role[4–5] — an interface between high-level intent and System 0, which may later be developed toward System 1 or distilled into System 0.

  • One interface. A single model for nominal motion, reactive recovery, and locomanipulation; recovery is generated, not selected by a fallen/standing automaton.
  • Closed on proprioception. Generation is trained from the same train/play rollout buffer as the policy; poorly tracked segments are rejected in simulation.
  • Fixed WBC contract. Upstream sources of intent can change without retraining the tracker.

A prototype of about 12M parameters already runs in the browser and on the ARM CPU of a Unitree G1 at 50 Hz. The sections that follow motivate the problem, describe the layer, present the current evidence, locate it in a stack, and outline a proposed roadmap.

1Motivation

Humanoid control has progressed from isolated skills — velocity tracking, balancing, or individual manipulation tasks — toward unified whole-body controllers. A single tracker, given an appropriate dense reference, can walk, run, dance, stand, and reproduce athletic skills. BeyondMimic, SONIC, and ZEST have made this paradigm increasingly standard[10, 12, 22]. The scarce component is therefore no longer the tracker itself, but a suitable and replaceable source of reference motion.

These references must still be generated — and generated quickly — from sparse intent such as joystick commands, full-body teleoperation, waypoints, or the output of a higher-level foundation model. Industrial architectures are already making this separation explicit. Helix, for example, splits slow semantic intent, a faster body-level policy, and a high-rate tracker[4–5]; GR00T Whole-Body Control drives a common WBC from motion clips, a kinematic planner, VR teleoperation, or a VLA[16]. Converting these sparse commands into agile, dynamically feasible, and trackable motion remains an open problem.

Over the past several years, generative modelling of human motion — and, more recently, flow matching[11] — has emerged as a natural tool for representing distributions of plausible human behaviour. Much of this development originated in game development and character animation and is now transferring to robotics. Motion Matching and Learned Motion Matching retrieve and blend clips rather than model a generative distribution[3, 9]. Control Operators is one of the clearest examples of flow matching already inside a game-development animation pipeline[7], and is one of the inspirations for this work.

Peng's line of physics-based character control is the closest conceptual relative: AMP and ASE introduced reusable motion manifolds[17–18]; CLoSD and GPC close a generative planner around a tracking controller, so get-up and perturbation recovery become ordinary generated motions[21, 23]; AMDM made generation autoregressive and interactive[20]. On robots, priors such as SMP, PFM-HR, CMP, and HMP bias RL and IK toward natural whole-body coordination[1, 6, 13–14], while Kimodo, ARDY, and MotionBricks write kinematic clips from text or constraints[19, 24, 26]. The Motion Layer takes the next step: the next WBC reference is sampled from a learned motion manifold while being conditioned directly on the live robot state.

Kinematics alone is insufficient for agile and robust motion generation. A nominal clip represents a world that can immediately become stale. It does not know that the robot is leaning, that a foot has slipped, that tracking error has accumulated, or that the IMU is biased. The motion manifold should be queried using the state of the physical system that is actually executing the motion, and it must be queried fast — at the tracker's 50–200 Hz rate and, ideally, on the same onboard CPU as the WBC. A foundation model can provide rich intent, but does not necessarily produce a dynamically trackable, state-aware whole-body reference.

2Approach

At each control step, the Motion Layer receives a short proprioceptive history — gyroscope measurements, projected gravity, joint positions and velocities, and optionally joint effort — together with the current sparse intent. It predicts the next WBC reference: base motion and a full-body pose, using the same reference interface already consumed by the tracker. The WBC remains frozen by default.

Closing generation on proprioception, while making it aware of the downstream tracker, is what distinguishes this approach from open-loop motion generation followed by tracking. A common architecture is an open-loop kinematic planner feeding a unified tracker — SONIC plus the GR00T kinematic planner is a current example[12, 16]. Kimodo, ARDY, and MotionBricks belong to the same family[19, 24, 26]: they write a kinematic clip without observing the robot that will execute it. Body lean, foot slip, tracker lag, and encoder noise are left entirely to the downstream WBC.

I propose the complementary architecture: close motion generation on proprioception and make the generator tracker-aware using the same simulation rollouts already collected by the control policy. At every step the Motion Layer conditions on where the physical system actually is and samples a reference that remains close to the human-motion manifold — a catch step or a get-up rather than the next frame of a kinematic clip.

The model is trained using the same rollout buffer already used for policy training and evaluation, without a separate dataset-storage pipeline. Latent flow matching is fitted to this buffer in a manner related to Control Operators[7, 11], but with different conditioning and outputs: proprioceptive history and sparse intent replace game-side control operators, while the model generates a WBC reference rather than a character-animation clip. The frozen tracker collects data under contact-rich interaction in GPU simulation (Isaac / Newton / MuJoCo Warp), using the same domain randomisation and sensor noise already employed during WBC training[25]. The collection pipeline is modular and can be attached to any stack that already trains a whole-body controller.

Switching generation onto this WBC experience — rather than onto kinematic clips — is what makes the simulated robot available as a source of reasoning: full state, contact forces, joint torques, and related dynamic signals. A mocap clone or an open-loop kinematic generator never observes these quantities and cannot condition, score, or filter motion on them.

Because the downstream tracker is present during data collection, poorly tracked segments can be rejected using only simulation and the tracking policy's own state. The generator is therefore trained preferentially on references that are not only human-like, but also executable by the target controller.

In the longer term, the same interface can accept joystick commands, full-body teleoperation, VLA motion chunks, or existing motion clips. A foundation model can remain responsible for language, perception, and long-horizon task reasoning; the Motion Layer is responsible for fast, trackable, state-aware whole-body motion. In simulation the same interface becomes a trajectory-generation engine. Because the model is small and naturally batched, it can later scale training data for both WBC and VLA systems.

3Proof of concept

As a proof of concept, I implemented an extensible latent flow-matching Motion Layer on top of a frozen wbc-mjlab tracker[7, 11, 15] and evaluated it in simulation and on a Unitree G1 (walk / run, disturbances). I maintain this tracker and use it here as a convenient host rather than as a required backend: any compatible controller can sit underneath the same reference contract without changing the generator. The prototype was trained on a subset of BONES-SEED[2] and LAFAN[8] — walking, crouching, running, and sitting — using the same train/play rollout buffer as the policy, without a separate storage pipeline, in the mjlab / MuJoCo Warp stack[25]. Poorly tracked segments are rejected from simulation and the tracking policy's state, with no extra labels.

The network is compact (~12M parameters). It conditions on a short history of onboard measurements already available on the robot — gyroscope, projected gravity, joint positions and velocities, motor current, and acceleration — together with style and sparse waypoints; the output is the standard reference already consumed by the tracker. All behaviours below are generated by this single model and tracked by a single frozen WBC, running together on the G1 ARM CPU at the policy rate (50 Hz).

One model generates references for a unified WBC.

The same cascade also runs on the G1 onboard CPU (walk / run teleoperation, disturbance rejection). A sparse key-body conditioning interface has also been implemented, but has not yet been evaluated on hardware. Vision is not required for the current prototype.

4Place in a stack

Many modern humanoid architectures already separate slow semantic intent, a faster body-level policy, and a high-rate tracking controller. Helix 02 is a convenient example of this decomposition, not a template that must be copied[4–5].

Within this notation, the Motion Layer occupies the interface between high-level intent and the tracker — an S0.5 layer that produces fast, proprioception-conditioned, trackable references. Each layer operates at its natural timescale.

The practical advantage is modularity. The upper VLA layers (S2 / S1) can be switched off, and the same Motion Layer still runs from joystick locomotion, torso or key-body tasks, teleoperation, and other sparse commands. That is often the more convenient way to collect data before a foundation model is ready, and it remains available afterwards as a fallback interface.

The present distinction from System 1 is observational: the Motion Layer does not yet use vision. That is a current choice, not a hard boundary — depth or local maps can be added later without changing the WBC contract. The proprioception at S0.5 is also not the same as System 0's. S0 typically needs only the high-rate joint and base state required to follow a reference; the Motion Layer can condition on a richer history, including motor current, acceleration, and other robot signals. Because this experience is collected in simulation, further modalities can be added without a new real-world instrumentation campaign.

Possible system evolution

Remain at S0.5

A standalone safety and intent-interface layer: sparse intent in; get-up, catch-step, and other reactive motions generated from live proprioception; difficult-to-track motion reshaped before reaching the WBC.

Grow toward System 1

Keep the same proprioceptive interface and add the missing visual channel — depth or local height maps — so a foundation model can condition a reactive target generator without collapsing the stack into one end-to-end network.

Distill to System 0

Jointly train or distill the generator into the WBC so that generative motion priors directly improve robustness, human-likeness, and responsiveness to sparse intent.

5Proposed roadmap

Click a stage to read it.

Now

Working proof of concept

  • A ~12M Motion Layer and a frozen tracker already run together on the G1 ARM CPU at 50 Hz.
  • Joystick / style locomotion and disturbance recovery are in place on the G1 (walk / run, disturbances); a key-body interface exists, but has not yet been evaluated on hardware.
  • The training pipeline is modular: the same setup can be reused for other tasks, other observations, and any humanoid that already has a WBC (wbc-mjlab[15] / other stack).
3–4 months

A working interface

  • Natural whole-body motion from joystick, waypoints, locomanipulation targets, or foundation-model tokens — “walk here”, “run 5 m forward”.
  • Transfer onto the target robot.
  • Generative full-body teleoperation from sparse points — physics-aware and human-like, without kinematic retarget.
  • Key-body tracking that is robust and agile — including dynamics, jumps, and acrobatics — with autonomous human-like disturbance rejection and fall recovery.
  • First integration with high-level VLA models.
4–8 months

Strengthening and expansion

  • Autonomous data-generation pipelines in simulation.
  • The Motion Layer runs stably on hardware with diverse intents, and can take on vision, height maps, and similar signals to generate obstacle traversal and stair walking.
  • Evolve toward System 0 — a more robust, human-like tracker conditioned on sparse intent — or toward System 1 by adding visual context.
Longer

Ordinary behaviour

  • A terrain-aware locomanipulation contract between any source of intent and the tracker, working in simulation and on hardware.
  • A simulation factory: known geometry and privileged goals automatically label contacts, recoveries, and style changes that mocap almost never covers.
